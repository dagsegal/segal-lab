Research

Mechanisms of Cell–Microenvironmental Interactions in Cancer

Cancer cell plasticity is a key driver of disease progression, enabling metastasis and resistance to therapy — yet the mechanisms by which cancer cells sense and adapt to external cues remain largely unknown. This gap is especially critical in pediatric cancers, where a low mutational burden beyond a single driver oncogene often contrasts sharply with the variability seen in patient outcomes, pointing to non-genetic, microenvironment-driven mechanisms of adaptation.

Our work centers on Ewing Sarcoma (EwS), a pediatric cancer of bone and soft tissue, as a prototype for studying these questions. Traditional multi-omics approaches capture transcriptional states but often miss the cell-biological processes — membrane dynamics, cytoskeletal reorganization, spatial compartmentalization of signaling — that actually drive cell function. We address this by developing and applying quantitative live-imaging approaches that resolve cancer cell behavior directly, in physiologically relevant contexts.

Zebrafish avatars

High-resolution, high-throughput live imaging of human cancer cells xenografted into distinct tissue microenvironments in a living vertebrate.

3D culture systems

Engineered extracellular matrix environments to dissect how specific cell–ECM interactions shape cell state and signaling.

Quantitative imaging

Custom analytical pipelines that turn subcellular imaging data into quantitative, morphology- and signaling-based readouts of cell state.

Current research directions

01

Chromatin remodeling and EWSR1⁠:⁠FLI1 activity

We are asking how chromatin state contributes to Ewing Sarcoma plasticity, beginning with how EWSR1:FLI1 (EF1) activity differs across microenvironments. Live imaging of histones and histone acetylation markers lets us follow nuclear changes as cells shift between environments. Building on this, we are designing transcriptional reporters of EF1 targets to visualize EF1 activity states in vivo, in zebrafish, across different tissue contexts.

02

ECM cues and cytoskeletal regulation

Changes in matrix composition and density alter cell shape, which shifts the polarization of Rac and with it the balance between cell migration and proliferation — a balance relevant to Ewing Sarcoma progression. We are perturbing Rac and Dia to dissect this pathway, defining the cytoskeletal regulators that translate ECM cues into cell state.

03

Membrane fluidity and cell state

We are profiling differences in membrane composition between CD99-high and CD99-low Ewing Sarcoma states, using membrane dyes followed by lipidomics. Differences we identify are genetically perturbed to test their effect on cell phenotype, nominating membrane-regulating pathways that could be targeted therapeutically.

04

Adhesion and tissue-specific adaptation

EwS cells form pseudorosettes — radially organized architectures seen clinically and in development — but only in specific tissue microenvironments, such as the zebrafish hindbrain ventricle. We are identifying the adhesion molecules that drive this tissue-specific self-organization, combining mosaic cell labeling with long-term light-sheet imaging to resolve their functional roles.

Together, these projects will define how microenvironmental cues orchestrate cell state transitions in cancer — building a foundation that extends beyond Ewing Sarcoma, and toward zebrafish patient-derived avatars for precision, context-aware cancer therapy.
